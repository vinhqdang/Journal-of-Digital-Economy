\title{Searching for the digital dividend: A debiased machine-learning sieve estimator for heterogeneous effects of digital adoption in panel data}

\author{Quang-Vinh Dang\corref{cor1}}
\ead{vinh.dq4@buv.edu.vn}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author. ORCID: 0000-0002-3877-8024.}
\affiliation{organization={British University Vietnam},
            city={Hung Yen},
            country={Vietnam}}

\begin{abstract}
Whether digital adoption raises productivity is usually assessed with two-way fixed-effects regressions that impose a single, linear effect and a linear control function. Both restrictions are at odds with theories in which the returns to digital technologies depend on absorptive capacity and distance to the technological frontier. This paper develops Panel-DOSE, a debiased orthogonal sieve estimator for panel data that recovers the whole function linking the effect of a continuous digital treatment to a moderating variable. The estimator combines Mundlak-augmented machine-learning nuisance functions cross-fitted by country, a two-way within transformation of the orthogonalised residuals, and a penalised B-spline sieve whose smoothness is chosen by generalised cross-validation. It delivers pointwise and simultaneous confidence bands, group-average effects and a one-degree-of-freedom test of heterogeneity. We show that the estimating equation is Neyman-orthogonal and exactly invariant to additive unit and time errors in the nuisance functions, and that, when the moderator is a control variable, only two nuisance functions are needed whatever the sieve dimension. In Monte Carlo experiments with nonlinear confounding, Panel-DOSE attains the lowest integrated error in every non-constant design, reducing it by 60--80\% relative to linear fixed-effects models and boosted R-learners. Applied to 141 countries over 1996--2025, the estimator finds no aggregate digital dividend: the average effect of internet adoption on subsequent labour-productivity growth is small, negative and insignificant, which rules out gains larger than about 0.13 percentage points per ten points of adoption, and it does not vary with schooling. Heterogeneity along the development dimension is not robust: its sign differs between World Bank data and the 10.0 vintage of the Penn World Table, and it vanishes in the 11.0 vintage for the same years, whereas a linear fixed-effects interaction reports it with spurious precision. Claims about where the digital dividend arrives should therefore be treated with caution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The expectation that digital technologies deliver a ``digital dividend'' in the form of faster productivity growth is central to development strategies and to much of the digital-economy literature \citep{worldbank2016wdr,goldfarb2019digital}. The empirical record is more mixed than this expectation suggests. Firm- and industry-level studies document large returns to information technology, but mainly where it is combined with organisational change and skilled labour \citep{brynjolfsson2000beyond,bresnahan2002it}. Cross-country evidence ranges from sizeable growth effects of broadband diffusion in advanced economies \citep{czernich2011broadband} to weak or insignificant effects in developing and emerging economies \citep{niebel2018ict,vu2020ict}. Recent contributions to this journal likewise emphasise that the economic consequences of digitalisation depend on structural and institutional conditions \citep{banga2026digitalisation,chen2026production}.

A common thread in these findings is heterogeneity. Classic growth theory predicts that the payoff from a new technology depends on the capacity to absorb it, whether this is captured by human capital \citep{nelson1966,benhabib1994,cohen1990absorptive} or by distance to the technological frontier \citep{acemoglu2006frontier,comin2010diffusion}. The productivity J-curve of \citet{brynjolfsson2021jcurve} adds that general-purpose technologies such as the internet require complementary intangible investment that depresses measured productivity before raising it. Taken together, these arguments imply that the effect of digital adoption is a \emph{function} of the adopting economy's characteristics, possibly non-monotone and possibly negative over part of its domain; arguments about ``leapfrogging'' point in the opposite direction, predicting the largest gains where information and transaction costs are initially highest.

The standard empirical toolkit is poorly suited to estimating such a function. Two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) regressions deliver one number and, when heterogeneity is modelled at all, do so through a linear interaction term. They also specify the control function linearly, although the determinants of both productivity growth and digital adoption---income, capital deepening, demography, openness---interact nonlinearly. When the control function is misspecified and correlated with adoption, the resulting bias contaminates the average effect and, more seriously, its apparent heterogeneity. Double/debiased machine learning \citep[DML;][]{chernozhukov2018dml} addresses the control-function problem, and its panel extensions \citep{clarke2023panel} deal with fixed effects, but they target a constant parameter. Methods for heterogeneous effects, such as causal forests and the R-learner \citep{wager2018forest,athey2019grf,nie2021quasi}, have been developed for cross-sectional data with binary treatments; they do not exploit the within-country variation that identifies effects in macro panels and they provide limited inference on the shape of the effect function.

This paper proposes an estimator that fills this gap. Panel-DOSE (\emph{Panel Debiased Orthogonal Sieve Estimator}) targets the varying-coefficient model
\begin{equation}
Y_{it} = \theta(Z_{it})\,D_{it} + g(X_{it}) + \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{it},
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
in which $D_{it}$ is a continuous digital-adoption measure, $Z_{it}$ is a moderator such as human capital or initial productivity, $g(\cdot)$ is an unknown control function and $(\alpha_i,\lambda_t)$ are country and year effects. The effect function $\theta(\cdot)$ is approximated by a penalised cubic B-spline sieve \citep{eilers1996}, and the sieve coefficients are estimated from a Neyman-orthogonal moment built on machine-learning residuals. The estimator has four ingredients. First, the nuisance learners are fed Mundlak (time-average) features, which absorb the part of the fixed effect that is correlated with observables \citep{mundlak1978,wooldridge2010}. Second, the nuisance functions are cross-fitted with folds formed by countries, so that no country's outcome is used to predict itself. Third, the cross-fitted residuals are two-way demeaned, which makes the moment exactly invariant to any additive country or year error in the machine-learning fits. Fourth, the smoothness of $\theta(\cdot)$ is chosen by generalised cross-validation, and inference relies on a cluster-robust sandwich, simultaneous (sup-$t$) bands \citep{montiel2019bands}, group-average effects and a best-linear-projection test of heterogeneity \citep{semenova2021cate}.

The paper makes three contributions. The first is methodological. We extend orthogonal sieve estimation of causal functions \citep{semenova2021cate} to fixed-effects panels with a continuous treatment, prove that the resulting moment is Neyman-orthogonal and invariant to additive nuisance errors, and establish asymptotic normality under conditions that are standard for DML with clustered data. We also show a computational property that is specific to the moderator being a control variable: all orthogonalised regressors can be generated from two nuisance functions, $\E[Y\mid\cdot]$ and $\E[D\mid\cdot]$, so the cost of the estimator does not grow with the sieve dimension and alternative sieves can be compared at no additional cost.

The second contribution is evidence on finite-sample performance. In a Monte Carlo study calibrated to a macro panel of 100 countries observed for 24 years, with fixed-effect and nonlinear confounding, Panel-DOSE attains the lowest integrated root-mean-squared error in all designs with a non-constant effect. It improves markedly on TWFE with linear interactions, on constant-effect panel DML, on a boosted R-learner applied to the same orthogonalised residuals, and on a pooled DML sieve that ignores the panel structure. The ablation shows that the fixed-effects treatment of the nuisance step, rather than machine learning per se, is responsible for most of the gain.

The third contribution is substantive. We apply the estimator to 141 economies over 1996--2025, combining World Bank productivity and ITU adoption data with UNDP schooling data, and compare the results with two vintages of the Penn World Table \citep{feenstra2015pwt}: version 11.0 for 1996--2023 and version 10.0 for 1996--2019. Conditional on flexible controls, the average effect of lagged internet adoption on labour-productivity growth is small, negative and statistically insignificant, and the confidence interval excludes the large effects often invoked in policy debates. The effect does not vary with schooling. Along the development dimension, the baseline estimates suggest larger effects in the least productive economies, but the gradient is not robust: PWT 10.0 data point to the opposite, a strongly negative effect in the least productive economies, and in PWT 11.0 for the same years the gradient is small and insignificant. The revision of the productivity data alone thus overturns a result that is significant at the 1\% level in the earlier vintage. A linear TWFE interaction, by contrast, reports a highly significant gradient, illustrating how a restrictive control function can manufacture precision. The results suggest that the digital dividend is not automatic and that evidence on where it arrives is more fragile than conventional estimates imply.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews the related literature. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the model, the Panel-DOSE algorithm and its properties. Section~\ref{sec:sim} reports the Monte Carlo evidence. Section~\ref{sec:emp} presents the empirical application, Section~\ref{sec:disc} discusses implications and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:concl} concludes. Proofs are collected in \ref{app:proofs} and data and implementation details in \ref{app:data}.

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:lit}

\subsection{Digital adoption, absorptive capacity and productivity}

Growth accounting attributes a large share of the US productivity resurgence of the late 1990s to the production and use of information technology \citep{jorgenson2008retro}. Outside the frontier, evidence on the productivity effects of digital technologies is less conclusive. \citet{czernich2011broadband} use an instrumental-variable approach based on pre-existing telecommunication networks and find that a ten-percentage-point increase in broadband penetration raised annual per-capita growth by 0.9--1.5 percentage points in OECD countries. \citet{niebel2018ict} finds that the output elasticity of ICT capital does not differ significantly between developing, emerging and developed economies, and that emerging and developing economies do not gain more from ICT investment than developed ones. \citet{vu2020ict} survey more than a hundred studies and conclude that the growth effect of ICT is positive on average but heterogeneous across countries, periods and technologies. Micro evidence points in the same direction: \citet{hjort2019internet} show that the arrival of fast internet in Africa increased employment, particularly in skilled occupations.

Several theories predict this heterogeneity. In \citet{nelson1966} and \citet{benhabib1994}, human capital governs the speed at which economies adopt and exploit new technologies; in the organisational literature, absorptive capacity plays the same role at the firm level \citep{cohen1990absorptive}. In \citet{acemoglu2006frontier}, the optimal growth strategy changes with distance to the frontier, so the same technology can have different returns at different stages of development. \citet{comin2010diffusion} document that adoption lags vary widely across countries and technologies, implying that the intensity of use, not just availability, determines the gains. \citet{brynjolfsson2021jcurve} formalise the J-curve: because general-purpose technologies need complementary intangible investment that is poorly measured, measured productivity growth first falls and then rises as the technology spreads. Measurement concerns have also been raised as an explanation for the post-2005 productivity slowdown, although \citet{syverson2017mismeasurement} and \citet{byrne2016mismeasurement} find that mismeasurement accounts for only a small part of it.

Empirically, heterogeneity is usually handled through sample splits by income group \citep{niebel2018ict}, linear interaction terms, or threshold regressions \citep{hansen1999threshold}. Sample splits are coarse and interactions impose linearity; threshold models impose a step function and are typically combined with linear controls. None of these approaches accommodates a flexible control function, and inference on the shape of the effect is usually limited to the coefficients of the chosen parametric form. Recent machine-learning contributions to this journal, for example on corporate carbon emissions \citep{xu2026carbon}, use flexible learners for prediction rather than for estimating causal or structural effects.

\subsection{Machine learning for causal and structural parameters}

Machine learning has become part of the applied econometrician's toolkit \citep{mullainathan2017ml,athey2019ml}. DML \citep{chernozhukov2018dml} generalises the partialling-out estimator of \citet{robinson1988}: it estimates the nuisance functions with any sufficiently accurate learner, uses cross-fitting to avoid overfitting bias and bases inference on a Neyman-orthogonal score that is insensitive to first-order errors in the nuisance estimates. \citet{clarke2023panel} adapt DML to static panels with fixed effects and compare correlated-random-effects, within-group and first-difference implementations. Their target is a constant coefficient.

Heterogeneous effects have been studied mainly in cross-sectional settings with binary treatments. Causal forests \citep{wager2018forest,athey2019grf} adapt random forests to estimate conditional average treatment effects; the R-learner of \citet{nie2021quasi} minimises a residual-on-residual loss with a flexible function class. \citet{semenova2021cate} show that projecting an orthogonal signal onto a growing series basis yields asymptotically normal estimates of causal functions and their best linear predictors. Varying-coefficient models \citep{hastie1993vc,cai2000fc} are the classical semiparametric counterpart, estimated by kernel or spline methods under parametric control functions. Series estimation theory \citep{chen2007sieve,belloni2015series} supplies the pointwise and uniform asymptotics for such sieves.

Panel-DOSE sits at the intersection of these strands. Relative to \citet{clarke2023panel}, it targets a function rather than a scalar and combines the correlated-random-effects and within-group strategies instead of choosing between them. Relative to \citet{semenova2021cate} and \citet{nie2021quasi}, it handles two-way fixed effects, cluster dependence and a continuous treatment, and exploits a structure-aware residualisation that keeps the cost independent of the sieve dimension. Relative to varying-coefficient and threshold models, it does not require the control function to be linear.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Model and target parameters}

We observe a panel $\{(Y_{it},D_{it},X_{it}) : i=1,\dots,G;\ t=1,\dots,T_i\}$ of $G$ countries, where $Y_{it}$ is an outcome (productivity growth), $D_{it}\in\mathbb{R}$ is a continuous digital-adoption measure, and $X_{it}\in\mathbb{R}^p$ collects controls that contains the moderator $Z_{it}$. We work with model~\eqref{eq:model}. The function $\theta(z)$ is the marginal effect of adoption on the outcome for an economy with moderator value $z$: a one-unit increase in $D$ changes $Y$ by $\theta(z)$, holding the controls and fixed effects constant. We also consider a dose-response variant,
\begin{equation}
Y_{it} = f(D_{it}) + g(X_{it}) + \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{it},
\label{eq:dose}
\end{equation}
whose target is the marginal effect $f'(d)$, which tells whether the returns to adoption diminish or rise with the level of adoption itself.

Three summaries of $\theta(\cdot)$ are useful in practice. The \emph{average effect} is $\bar\theta=\E[\theta(Z_{it})]$. The \emph{group-average effect} for a set $A$ of moderator values is $\bar\theta_A=\E[\theta(Z_{it})\mid Z_{it}\in A]$; we use the terciles of $Z$. The \emph{best linear projection} (BLP) slope $\gamma$ solves $\min_{a,\gamma}\E[(\theta(Z_{it})-a-\gamma Z_{it})^2]$ and gives a one-degree-of-freedom summary of how the effect changes with the moderator \citep{semenova2021cate}.

\subsection{Identification}

Write $F_{it}=(X_{it},\bar X_i,\bar D_i,t)$ for the controls augmented by their country averages $\bar X_i=T_i^{-1}\sum_t X_{it}$ and $\bar D_i=T_i^{-1}\sum_t D_{it}$, and a time index.

\begin{assumption}[Conditional strict exogeneity]\label{as:exo}
$\E[\varepsilon_{it}\mid D_{i1},\dots,D_{iT_i},X_{i1},\dots,X_{iT_i},\alpha_i]=0$ for all $t$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Correlated random effects in the nuisance step]\label{as:cre}
$\E[\alpha_i\mid F_{it}]=h(\bar X_i,\bar D_i)$ for some function $h$ that does not depend on $t$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Nuisance sufficiency]\label{as:suff}
$\E[\Phi_{it}\mid F_{i1},\dots,F_{iT_i}]=\E[\Phi_{it}\mid F_{it}]$ for all $t$.
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{as:exo} is the usual strict-exogeneity condition of static fixed-effects models; in the application it motivates entering digital adoption with a lag and it is discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:disc}. Assumption~\ref{as:cre} is a nonparametric version of the Mundlak device: the conditional mean of the country effect given the augmented controls may be any function of the country averages \citep{mundlak1978,wooldridge2010}. It is weaker than the linear Mundlak projection, and the part of $\alpha_i$ that it does not explain, $a_i=\alpha_i-h(\bar X_i,\bar D_i)$, is removed by the within transformation described below. Assumption~\ref{as:suff} requires that the augmented controls of period $t$ summarise the information in the whole control path that is relevant for predicting adoption; the Mundlak averages in $F_{it}$ are included precisely to carry this country-level information. Finally, $D_{it}$ must retain variation after conditioning on $F_{it}$ and removing country and year means; this overlap condition is formalised as the invertibility of the matrix $J$ in Assumption~\ref{as:reg}.

Let $b(z)=(b_1(z),\dots,b_K(z))'$ be a cubic B-spline basis with interior knots at quantiles of $Z$ and write $\theta(z)=b(z)'\beta+r_K(z)$ with approximation error $r_K$. Define the $K$ ``sieve regressors'' $\Phi_{it}=D_{it}\,b(Z_{it})$ and the nuisance functions $\ell(F)=\E[Y\mid F]$ and $m(F)=\E[\Phi\mid F]$. Let $\M$ be the two-way within operator that removes country and year means from a variable (for unbalanced panels, by alternating projections). The population moment is
\begin{equation}
\psi(\beta;\ell,m)=\E\Big[\sum_t \M\big(\Phi-m(F)\big)_{it}\,\Big\{\M\big(Y-\ell(F)\big)_{it}-\M\big(\Phi-m(F)\big)_{it}'\beta\Big\}\Big]=0 .
\label{eq:moment}
\end{equation}
Under Assumptions~\ref{as:exo}--\ref{as:suff} and $r_K=0$, $Y-\ell(F)-(\Phi-m(F))'\beta=a_i+\varepsilon_{it}$, because $\lambda_t$ is a function of the time index in $F$ and is absorbed by $\ell$. The term $a_i$ is removed by $\M$ and $\varepsilon_{it}$ is mean-independent of the whole path of $F$, so the true $\beta$ solves \eqref{eq:moment}.

\subsection{Two structural properties}

\begin{proposition}[Orthogonality and fixed-effect invariance]\label{prop:orth}
Let $\psi$ be defined by~\eqref{eq:moment} and let Assumptions~\ref{as:exo}--\ref{as:suff} hold.
(i) \emph{Neyman orthogonality:} for any directions $(\delta_\ell,\delta_m)$ in the nuisance space, $\partial_r\psi(\beta_0;\ell_0+r\delta_\ell,m_0+r\delta_m)\big|_{r=0}=0$.
(ii) \emph{Exact invariance:} for any country-specific and year-specific constants $(c_i,c_t)$ and $(d_i,d_t)\in\mathbb{R}^K$, $\psi(\beta;\ell+c_i+c_t,\,m+d_i+d_t)=\psi(\beta;\ell,m)$ for all $\beta$.
\end{proposition}

Part (i) is the property that makes DML insensitive to first-order errors in the machine-learning fits. Part (ii) is specific to the panel construction: any error of the learners that takes the form of a country or year shift---a natural failure mode when trees approximate a fixed effect through country averages---has no effect on the estimate at all, so the product-rate condition of DML only needs to hold for the within-country, within-year component of the nuisance error. Section~\ref{sec:sim} shows that this matters in finite samples.

\begin{proposition}[Structure-aware residualisation]\label{prop:struct}
If $Z_{it}$ is an element of $F_{it}$, then $m_k(F)=b_k(Z)\,\mu(F)$ for $k=1,\dots,K$, where $\mu(F)=\E[D\mid F]$. Hence the orthogonalised sieve regressors are $b(Z_{it})\{D_{it}-\mu(F_{it})\}$, and the estimator requires only the two nuisance functions $\ell$ and $\mu$, whatever the sieve dimension $K$.
\end{proposition}

Proposition~\ref{prop:struct} follows from $\E[D\,b_k(Z)\mid F]=b_k(Z)\E[D\mid F]$ when $Z$ is measurable with respect to $F$. It reduces the number of learners from $K+1$ to two, avoids the instability of fitting $K$ separate regressions for highly collinear spline regressors, and allows the sieve (number of knots, penalty, or a linear basis for the BLP) to be changed after the nuisance step without refitting. In the dose-response model~\eqref{eq:dose}, $\Phi$ is a basis in $D$ and the $K$ conditional means are learned separately.

\subsection{The Panel-DOSE algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:dose} summarises the estimator. The final stage minimises a penalised least-squares criterion in the orthogonalised variables,
\begin{equation}
\hat\beta_\lambda=\argmin_\beta \ \sum_{i,t}\big(\tilde Y_{it}-\tilde\Phi_{it}'\beta\big)^2+\lambda\,n\,\beta' P\beta,\qquad P=\Delta_2'\Delta_2,
\label{eq:pls}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde Y=\M(Y-\hat\ell)$, $\tilde\Phi=\M(\Phi-\hat m)$, $n=\sum_iT_i$, and $\Delta_2$ is the second-difference matrix of the P-spline approach of \citet{eilers1996}. Because the null space of $P$ contains the coefficients of linear functions, a large $\lambda$ shrinks $\theta(\cdot)$ towards a linear function rather than towards zero. The penalty is chosen by generalised cross-validation \citep{craven1979gcv}, $\mathrm{GCV}(\lambda)=n\,\mathrm{RSS}(\lambda)/\{n-\mathrm{tr}(H_\lambda)\}^2$, with $H_\lambda=\tilde\Phi(\tilde\Phi'\tilde\Phi+\lambda nP)^{-1}\tilde\Phi'$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Panel-DOSE}\label{alg:dose}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Panel $(Y_{it},D_{it},X_{it},Z_{it})$; learner $\mathcal{L}$; folds $L$; repetitions $S$; knots $q$
\State Build $F_{it}=(X_{it},\bar X_i,\bar D_i,\bar Z_i,t)$ and the B-spline basis $b(\cdot)$ with $q$ interior knots at quantiles of $Z$
\For{$s=1,\dots,S$}
  \State Randomly partition the \emph{countries} into $L$ folds $I_1,\dots,I_L$
  \For{$l=1,\dots,L$}
    \State Fit $\hat\ell^{(-l)}$ and $\hat\mu^{(-l)}$ by $\mathcal{L}$ on countries not in $I_l$
    \State For $i\in I_l$: $\hat e^Y_{it}=Y_{it}-\hat\ell^{(-l)}(F_{it})$, \ $\hat e^\Phi_{it}=b(Z_{it})\{D_{it}-\hat\mu^{(-l)}(F_{it})\}$
  \EndFor
  \State Two-way demean: $\tilde Y=\M\hat e^Y$, $\tilde\Phi=\M\hat e^\Phi$
  \State Choose $\hat\lambda_s$ by GCV and compute $\hat\beta_s$ from~\eqref{eq:pls}
  \State Compute the cluster-robust covariance $\hat V_s$ from~\eqref{eq:vcov}
\EndFor
\State $\hat\beta=\mathrm{median}_s\,\hat\beta_s$; \ $\hat V=\mathrm{median}_s\{\hat V_s+(\hat\beta_s-\hat\beta)(\hat\beta_s-\hat\beta)'\}$
\State \Return $\hat\theta(z)=b(z)'\hat\beta$, bands, group averages and BLP slope
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Cross-fitting by countries rather than by observations is essential in a panel. With observation-level folds, the learner is trained on other years of the same country, so the fitted value of $Y_{it}$ partly reproduces its own country effect and the residuals are too small; country-level folds keep the training and evaluation samples independent under cluster sampling. Repeating the split $S$ times and taking the median, with the variance adjustment of \citet{chernozhukov2018dml}, removes the dependence of the estimate on a particular partition.

\subsection{Inference}

Let $A_\lambda=\tilde\Phi'\tilde\Phi+\lambda nP$ and $\hat u_{it}=\tilde Y_{it}-\tilde\Phi_{it}'\hat\beta$. The cluster-robust covariance estimator is
\begin{equation}
\hat V=c_n\,A_\lambda^{-1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^G \hat s_i\hat s_i'\Big)A_\lambda^{-1},\qquad \hat s_i=\sum_t\tilde\Phi_{it}\hat u_{it},
\label{eq:vcov}
\end{equation}
with the small-sample factor $c_n=\frac{G}{G-1}\frac{n-1}{n-K}$ \citep{cameron2015cluster}. For any linear functional $\omega(\beta)=w'\beta$---the value $\hat\theta(z)=b(z)'\hat\beta$, the average effect with $w=n^{-1}\sum_{it}b(Z_{it})$, a group average, or a difference of group averages---the standard error is $(w'\hat Vw)^{1/2}$. Simultaneous bands over a grid $z_1,\dots,z_J$ use the critical value $c_{1-a}$ equal to the $(1-a)$ quantile of $\max_j|\xi_j|$, where $\xi\sim N(0,\hat\Sigma)$ and $\hat\Sigma$ is the correlation matrix of $\{\hat\theta(z_j)\}$ \citep{montiel2019bands}. The BLP slope is computed by re-solving the final stage with a linear basis $b(z)=(1,z)'$, which by Proposition~\ref{prop:struct} requires no new nuisance fits.

\begin{assumption}[Regularity]\label{as:reg}
(a) Countries are independent, $G\to\infty$, and $T_i\le\bar T<\infty$. (b) The cross-fitted nuisance estimators satisfy $\|\hat\mu-\mu\|_2\,(\|\hat\mu-\mu\|_2+\|\hat\ell-\ell\|_2)=o_p(G^{-1/2})$ and $\|\hat\ell-\ell\|_2=o_p(1)$, where by Proposition~\ref{prop:orth}(ii) the norms need only apply to the within-transformed errors. (c) $J=\E[\sum_t\tilde\Phi^0_{it}\tilde\Phi^{0\prime}_{it}]$ is positive definite, where $\tilde\Phi^0$ uses the true nuisance functions, and fourth moments are bounded. (d) $K$ is fixed and $\lambda_G\sqrt G\to0$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{proposition}[Asymptotic normality]\label{prop:an}
Under Assumptions~\ref{as:exo}--\ref{as:reg} and the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:struct}, $\sqrt G(\hat\beta-\beta_K)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,J^{-1}\Omega J^{-1})$, where $\beta_K$ is the sieve pseudo-true value and $\Omega=\E[s_is_i']$ with $s_i=\sum_t\tilde\Phi^0_{it}u_{it}$, and $G\hat V\xrightarrow{p}J^{-1}\Omega J^{-1}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{prop:an} treats the sieve dimension as fixed, so $\hat\theta(z)$ is centred at the best sieve approximation $b(z)'\beta_K$. Inference on $\theta(z)$ itself requires $K\to\infty$ with the approximation bias negligible relative to the standard error; the conditions of \citet{belloni2015series} and \citet{semenova2021cate} for series projections of orthogonal signals then deliver pointwise and uniform validity. The average effect, group averages and the BLP slope are smooth functionals that do not require undersmoothing. In practice, the penalty selected by GCV trades a small smoothing bias for a large variance reduction; Section~\ref{sec:sim} quantifies the effect of this choice on coverage.
\end{remark}

\section{Monte Carlo evidence}\label{sec:sim}

\subsection{Design}

The simulation mimics a macro panel of $G=100$ countries observed for $T=24$ years. Each country has a fixed effect $\alpha_i\sim N(0,1)$ and there is a random-walk year effect. Five controls follow $X_{it}=0.6X_{i,t-1}+0.4(\eta_{it}+0.5\alpha_i)$ with $\eta_{it}\sim N(0,I_5)$, so they are persistent and correlated with the fixed effect. The moderator $Z_{it}\in[0,1]$ trends slowly around a country-specific level. Digital adoption is a logistic diffusion index,
\[
D_{it}=\Lambda\big(-3+0.22t+0.6\alpha_i+0.8\sin(1.5X_{1it})+0.6X_{2it}\mathbb{1}\{X_{3it}>0\}+1.5Z_{it}+v_{it}\big),
\]
where $\Lambda$ is the logistic function and $v_{it}\sim N(0,1)$,
and the outcome is $Y_{it}=\theta(Z_{it})D_{it}+g(X_{it},Z_{it})+\alpha_i+\lambda_t+\varepsilon_{it}$ with $\varepsilon_{it}\sim N(0,0.25)$ and the nonlinear control function
\[
g=\rho\big\{1.5\sin(1.5X_{1it})+X_{2it}\mathbb{1}\{X_{3it}>0\}+0.5|X_{4it}|+3.2(Z_{it}-0.5)^2\big\}.
\]
Adoption and the outcome therefore share country effects, a time trend and nonlinear functions of the controls. We consider four effect functions: \emph{constant} $\theta(z)=1$; \emph{linear} $\theta(z)=-0.5+3z$; \emph{threshold} $\theta(z)=-0.5+2.5/(1+e^{-12(z-0.5)})$, a smooth J-curve-type transition; and \emph{hump} $\theta(z)=2-8(z-0.5)^2$. The baseline sets $\rho=1$; sensitivity analyses vary the confounding strength ($\rho\in\{0.5,2\}$) and the number of countries ($G\in\{50,200\}$) for the threshold design.

We compare seven estimators: (1) \emph{TWFE}, a linear two-way fixed-effects regression with a constant effect; (2) \emph{TWFE-interaction}, which adds $D\times Z$ and $D\times Z^2$; (3) \emph{FE-DML (constant)}, the panel DML estimator with Mundlak features, country-level cross-fitting and within transformation, which is Panel-DOSE with a single basis function, in the spirit of \citet{clarke2023panel}; (4) \emph{FE-DML + R-learner}, which fits $\theta(\cdot)$ to the same orthogonalised residuals with gradient boosting and the R-learner loss \citep{nie2021quasi}; (5) \emph{Pooled DML-sieve}, the sieve estimator of \citet{semenova2021cate} applied to the pooled data without Mundlak features or within transformation; (6) \emph{Panel-DOSE (unpenalised)}; and (7) \emph{Panel-DOSE}. All machine-learning estimators use the same LightGBM learner \citep{ke2017lightgbm} with 600 trees, five country-level folds and, for the sieve estimators, cubic B-splines with four interior knots and two repetitions of the sample split. Performance is measured on a grid of 17 points in $[0.1,0.9]$ by the integrated root-mean-squared error $\mathrm{IRMSE}=\{J^{-1}\sum_j(\hat\theta(z_j)-\theta(z_j))^2\}^{1/2}$ and the integrated bias, by the bias and RMSE of the average effect, and by the coverage of nominal 95\% pointwise and simultaneous bands. Each design is replicated 100 times (50 in the sensitivity analyses).

\subsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:sim} and Figure~\ref{fig:simirmse} report the results for the baseline designs. Four findings stand out.

First, the linear fixed-effects estimators are severely biased. Because the control function is nonlinear and correlated with adoption, TWFE and TWFE-interaction overstate the effect by 0.5--0.9 units on average, and their confidence intervals almost never cover the truth. The interaction model does not help even when the true $\theta(\cdot)$ is exactly quadratic (the constant, linear and hump designs, in which its errors are identical because the effect function lies in the span of its regressors): its problem is the control function, not the flexibility of the effect.

Second, handling the panel structure in the nuisance step is essential. The pooled DML sieve uses the same learner and the same sieve as Panel-DOSE but neither Mundlak features nor the within transformation. Its integrated bias is about 0.6 in every design, of the same order as that of TWFE, because the learners cannot separate the country effects from the controls and the orthogonalised treatment still carries the correlation between adoption and $\alpha_i$. Once the Mundlak features and the within step are added, the integrated bias falls to 0.05--0.07. The remaining bias is common to all panel-DML estimators and reflects the finite-sample regularisation bias of the nuisance learners; it is small relative to the effect.

Third, among the estimators that handle fixed effects, the sieve dominates the alternatives whenever the effect is heterogeneous. Constant-effect FE-DML is the best estimator when the effect is in fact constant, but its IRMSE is two to five times that of Panel-DOSE in the linear, threshold and hump designs. The boosted R-learner, which fits $\theta(\cdot)$ to the same residuals, has an IRMSE of about 0.58 in every design because the residual-on-residual regression is noisy and trees approximate smooth functions poorly with a few thousand observations. Panel-DOSE reduces the integrated error by 73--81\% relative to TWFE-interaction and by 61--73\% relative to the R-learner in the three heterogeneous designs, and attains the lowest IRMSE in each of them. The penalty also pays off: relative to the unpenalised sieve, GCV reduces the IRMSE by 14--40\% in the heterogeneous designs. Figure~\ref{fig:simcurves} illustrates these differences for one sample per design.

Fourth, inference is reasonably reliable. The pointwise 95\% intervals of the unpenalised sieve cover the true function at 93--94\% of grid points, and those of the penalised estimator at 84--93\%; the shortfall of the penalised version is largest in the threshold design, where the data-driven penalty induces smoothing bias around the steep part of the curve. Simultaneous bands cover the entire function in 70--88\% of replications. All panel-DML estimators estimate the average effect with an RMSE between 0.07 and 0.11, against more than 0.5 for the linear and pooled estimators.

Table~\ref{tab:simsens} shows that the ranking is robust. For the threshold design, Panel-DOSE has the lowest IRMSE for every combination of sample size and confounding strength. Its error falls from 0.35 with 50 countries to 0.15 with 200, roughly in line with root-$G$ convergence of the sieve coefficients, whereas the pooled DML sieve and TWFE-interaction remain biased as $G$ grows. Doubling the strength of nonlinear confounding nearly doubles the error of TWFE-interaction but raises that of Panel-DOSE only from 0.23 to 0.29.

\section{Empirical application: internet adoption and productivity growth, 1996--2025}\label{sec:emp}

\subsection{Data and specification}

The main data set combines the World Bank's World Development Indicators (WDI, July 2026 release) with the UNDP Human Development Report 2025. The outcome is annual growth of GDP per person employed at constant 2021 PPP prices, $Y_{it}=100\,\Delta\ln(\text{GDP}_{it}/\text{EMP}_{it})$, which WDI compiles from national accounts and the International Labour Organization's modelled employment estimates and which is available up to 2025. The treatment is the share of the population using the internet (ITU) in year $t-1$, measured on $[0,1]$, so that $\theta(z)$ is the change in growth (in percentage points) associated with moving from no adoption to universal adoption; one tenth of it is the effect of a ten-percentage-point increase. Controls, all lagged one year, are log GDP per worker (conditional convergence), mean years of schooling, the gross capital formation and government consumption shares of GDP, trade openness (exports plus imports over GDP), population growth, the age-dependency ratio, the urban population share, lagged productivity growth, and mobile and fixed broadband subscriptions per capita; they follow the empirical growth literature \citep{barro1991,mankiw1992}. Mean years of schooling is available up to 2023; its 2024 value, which enters only the 2025 observations, is extrapolated from each country's 2019--2023 trend. The sample covers 1996--2025, drops growth observations below the 1st and above the 99th percentile, and keeps countries with at least 15 usable years. The resulting unbalanced panel contains 3,806 observations on 141 countries, listed in \ref{app:data}. Table~\ref{tab:desc} reports descriptive statistics.

To connect with the growth-accounting literature, we also use the Penn World Table \citep{feenstra2015pwt}, which provides capital stocks, a human-capital index and TFP but ends earlier than WDI. The main PWT panel uses version 11.0 \citep{pwt11} and contains 3,087 observations on 117 countries over 1996--2023. A second panel uses version 10.0, which ends in 2019, and contains 2,566 observations on 113 countries. Both use PWT labour productivity (real GDP at constant national prices per person engaged) as the outcome and add log capital per worker and the labour share to the controls. They serve as robustness checks and, because they cover the same years with different vintages of the underlying national accounts and price data, they also allow us to ask whether conclusions depend on the source and vintage of the productivity data.

Figure~\ref{fig:desc} shows the variation that identifies the effects. Internet use diffused in all groups of countries, but at very different times: in the countries with the highest initial productivity, about half of the population was online by the mid-2000s, whereas in the least productive third the share reached 20\% only in the mid-2010s and passed one half at the end of the sample. Productivity growth was highest in the low and middle groups during the 2000s, collapsed in 2009 and 2020, rebounded in 2010 and 2021, and was lowest in the high group in most years. Because common shocks such as the global financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic are absorbed by year effects, the estimates rely on differences across countries in the timing of adoption relative to their own productivity paths, conditional on the controls.

We estimate model~\eqref{eq:model} with two moderators that correspond to the two mechanisms discussed in Section~\ref{sec:lit}: mean years of schooling (absorptive capacity) and lagged log GDP per worker (distance to the frontier). We also estimate the dose-response model~\eqref{eq:dose} in internet penetration. Panel-DOSE uses LightGBM with 400 trees, five country-level folds, five repetitions of the split and cubic B-splines with four interior knots; standard errors are clustered by country. We report the average effect, the effects averaged within terciles of the moderator (low, middle, high), the high-minus-low difference, and the BLP slope.

\subsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:emp} and Figure~\ref{fig:emp} summarise the estimates.

\paragraph{Average effect}
There is no evidence of an aggregate digital dividend in labour-productivity growth. TWFE with the same controls yields $-1.23$ (s.e.\ 0.77), constant-effect FE-DML $-1.11$ (1.14), and the Panel-DOSE average effect in the baseline specification is $-0.94$ (1.13). The last estimate implies that a ten-percentage-point increase in internet users changes subsequent annual productivity growth by $-0.09$ percentage points, with a 95\% confidence interval of $[-0.32,\,0.13]$. The estimates are therefore precise enough to rule out positive effects larger than about 0.13 percentage points per ten points of adoption, an order of magnitude smaller than the broadband effects that \citet{czernich2011broadband} estimate for OECD countries. The average is negative and insignificant in every WDI-based specification of Panel~A, including the pre-COVID period 1996--2019 ($-1.75$, s.e.\ 1.51) and the sample that excludes 2020--2021 ($-1.01$, s.e.\ 1.11), so the pandemic years do not drive the result. The PWT panels agree: the average is $-1.91$ (s.e.\ 1.47) in PWT 11.0 for 1996--2023, $-1.48$ (1.84) in PWT 11.0 for 1996--2019 and $-3.11$ (2.01) in PWT 10.0, all insignificant, and restricting the WDI data to the countries and years of the PWT 11.0 panel gives $-1.44$ (1.72). The absence of an average dividend is therefore robust to the source and vintage of the data.

\paragraph{Schooling}
With mean years of schooling as the moderator (Panel~A of Table~\ref{tab:emp} and Figure~\ref{fig:emp}a), the estimated function is flat and close to zero: it lies between $-2.3$ and $1.1$ over the 5th to 95th percentiles of schooling (2.6 to 13.0 years), the high-minus-low difference is 1.15 (s.e.\ 2.74), the BLP slope is 0.13 (s.e.\ 0.38) per year of schooling, and the simultaneous band contains a constant function. The absence of heterogeneity holds across learners, sieves, lags, samples and data sources (Figure~\ref{fig:emprob}). The only specification in which the difference reaches the 10\% level is the one without Mundlak features, which the simulations show to be biased.

\paragraph{Distance to the frontier}
With lagged log GDP per worker as the moderator (Panel~B and Figure~\ref{fig:emp}b), the evidence is more intriguing but fragile. In the baseline specification the average effect is positive in the least productive third of country-years ($7.42$, s.e.\ 2.97) and negative in the most productive third ($-2.07$, s.e.\ 1.87), and the difference of $-9.49$ (s.e.\ 3.46) is significant at the 1\% level. This is the pattern predicted by ``leapfrogging'' arguments, in which the internet lowers information and transaction costs most where they are initially highest. Three observations, however, counsel against taking it at face value. First, the estimated function is not monotone: it oscillates between about $-4.5$ and $4.4$ below 25,000 dollars per worker, the BLP slope ($-2.20$, s.e.\ 1.39) is not significant, and the 95\% simultaneous band contains a constant function. Second, the tercile difference keeps its negative sign in all WDI-based robustness checks but shrinks to between $-3.0$ and $-7.4$ and is not significant in any of them. Third, and most tellingly, the result depends on the productivity data. Restricting the WDI data to the countries and years of the PWT 11.0 panel gives a similar difference ($-8.77$, s.e.\ 4.26), so the sample does not explain it. With PWT 11.0 itself, however, the difference is small, positive and insignificant, both for 1996--2023 ($2.55$, s.e.\ 3.37) and for 1996--2019 ($3.11$, s.e.\ 4.14), and it is close to zero for TFP growth ($1.29$, s.e.\ 3.61). With PWT 10.0 for 1996--2019 the pattern is reversed and strong: the effect is $-17.19$ (s.e.\ 5.54) in the least productive third and close to zero in the most productive third, a difference of $+17.50$ (s.e.\ 5.84) that is significant at the 1\% level. The comparison of the two PWT vintages over identical years is the cleanest test of fragility: a revision of the underlying national accounts and price data between the two releases is enough to turn a highly significant J-curve pattern into a null result.

The linear benchmark illustrates why this matters. A TWFE regression with a linear interaction between internet use and log GDP per worker produces an interaction coefficient of $-1.91$ with a standard error of 0.52 ($t\approx3.7$), that is, apparently strong evidence that the returns to adoption fall with development. The debiased estimates show that this precision is largely an artefact of the linear control function: once the controls enter flexibly, the gradient is imprecisely estimated, it is not robust to reasonable changes in the specification, and its sign and significance depend on the source and vintage of the data.

\paragraph{Dose response}
The dose-response model (Figure~\ref{fig:emp}c) asks whether the returns to adoption change with the level of adoption itself. The penalty selected by GCV shrinks $f$ to an almost linear function, and the estimated marginal effect $\hat f'(d)$ is within 0.1 of zero over the whole range from the 20th to the 95th percentile of internet penetration; the 95\% simultaneous band, whose half-width is about one percentage point, contains the zero function. There is thus no indication of either increasing returns (network effects) or diminishing returns (saturation) to internet adoption in aggregate productivity growth.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{Interpretation}

Three features of the empirical results deserve emphasis. First, over three decades and 141 economies, the diffusion of the internet has not been followed by faster measured labour-productivity growth. The point estimates are small and negative, and they are precise enough to exclude effects of the size often invoked in policy discussions. This is consistent with the aggregate productivity paradox and with the J-curve argument of \citet{brynjolfsson2021jcurve}: if the gains require complementary intangible investment that is poorly measured, the measured productivity dividend of a general-purpose technology may be small for a long time. It is also consistent with \citet{niebel2018ict} and with the heterogeneous findings surveyed by \citet{vu2020ict}. Access, as measured by the share of internet users, is a coarse proxy for the intensity and quality of use, and the gains may accrue through channels---consumer surplus, variety, time savings---that productivity statistics do not capture \citep{byrne2016mismeasurement,syverson2017mismeasurement}.

Second, formal schooling does not condition the effect. The theory of absorptive capacity \citep{nelson1966,benhabib1994,cohen1990absorptive} predicts larger returns in better-educated economies, but the estimated function is flat across the whole distribution of schooling and the data reject even moderate gradients. Years of schooling may capture the skills that are complementary to digital technologies only imperfectly \citep{sianesi2003education}, and complementarities may operate through managerial and organisational capabilities that aggregate data do not measure.

Third, heterogeneity along the development dimension is not robustly identified. Depending on the productivity data, the estimates suggest leapfrogging (larger effects in low-productivity economies, WDI), a J-curve in which the least productive economies experience a transitional drag (PWT 10.0), or no gradient at all (PWT 11.0). Neither pattern survives the full set of checks, and the linear TWFE interaction that seems to settle the question owes its precision to a restrictive control function. We view this as the main substantive lesson of the application: conclusions about where the digital dividend arrives are sensitive to data vintages and specification choices, and flexible estimators with honest uncertainty quantification are needed to reveal that sensitivity.

\subsection{Policy implications}

The results caution against treating connectivity targets as productivity policy. Across the income distribution, expanding internet access has not been followed by faster measured productivity growth within the horizon we observe. This does not imply that such investments are undesirable, since connectivity has consumer-surplus, inclusion and service-delivery benefits that productivity statistics do not capture \citep{worldbank2016wdr,goldfarb2019digital}. It does imply that a productivity dividend, if it exists, is contingent on complementary investment---in firm capabilities, management practices, skills and the reallocation of resources towards digitally intensive activities---and that policies promoting access should be evaluated on their own merits rather than justified by expected aggregate productivity gains. The fragility of the heterogeneity estimates also suggests that governments should be wary of cross-country benchmarks that claim to identify the stage of development at which digital investment pays off.

\subsection{Limitations}

The analysis has limitations that also point to extensions. The identifying assumptions are those of static fixed-effects models augmented by the correlated-random-effects restriction in the nuisance step. Strict exogeneity rules out feedback from productivity shocks to future adoption; lagging the treatment by one or two years mitigates reverse causality but does not eliminate it, and the inclusion of lagged productivity growth is subject to a Nickell-type bias that is small with $T\approx 27$ but not zero \citep{nickell1981}. Results are, however, similar when lagged growth is excluded. An instrumental-variable version of Panel-DOSE, in which the orthogonalised treatment is replaced by an orthogonalised instrument in the spirit of \citet{czernich2011broadband}, is a natural extension. Second, the most recent observations rest on provisional national accounts and on the ILO's modelled employment estimates, and the 2024 value of schooling is extrapolated. The similarity of the full-sample and pre-COVID estimates suggests that these data issues do not drive the average results, but the comparison of PWT vintages shows that data revisions can change conclusions about heterogeneity. Third, internet penetration measures access rather than the intensity or quality of use; measures of firm-level digital adoption or data-intensive investment would sharpen the interpretation. Fourth, the asymptotic theory treats the sieve dimension as fixed, so the confidence bands for $\theta(z)$ are centred at the best spline approximation; the simulation evidence indicates that the smoothing bias induced by the data-driven penalty can reduce pointwise coverage for sharply nonlinear functions, and the unpenalised version or low-dimensional functionals (group averages, BLP slopes) should be preferred when coverage is paramount. Finally, as with any aggregate analysis, the estimates describe country-level associations and need not carry over to firms or regions.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}

This paper has proposed Panel-DOSE, an estimator for the function that links the effect of a continuous digital treatment to the characteristics of the adopting economy in fixed-effects panels. The estimator combines Mundlak-augmented machine-learning nuisance functions, cross-fitting by country, a two-way within transformation of the orthogonalised residuals and a penalised spline sieve. Its estimating equation is Neyman-orthogonal and exactly invariant to additive country and year errors in the nuisance fits, and when the moderator is a control variable the estimator needs only two nuisance functions whatever the flexibility of the sieve. The procedure provides the effect function with pointwise and simultaneous bands, group-average effects and a best-linear-projection summary, and it is simple to implement with off-the-shelf learners.

The Monte Carlo evidence shows that these ingredients matter. Linear fixed-effects models are badly biased when the control function is nonlinear, pooled machine-learning estimators that ignore the panel structure inherit the bias of the fixed effects, and a boosted R-learner applied to the same residuals is noisier than the sieve. Panel-DOSE attains the lowest integrated error in every design with a heterogeneous effect.

Applied to 141 economies over 1996--2025, the estimator finds no aggregate digital dividend in labour-productivity growth: the average effect of internet adoption is small, negative and insignificant, and it does not vary with schooling. Heterogeneity along the development dimension appears in some specifications but changes sign with the source of the productivity data and disappears in the latest vintage of the Penn World Table, whereas a linear fixed-effects interaction reports it with spurious precision. The digital dividend, in other words, does not arrive automatically with connectivity, and claims about where it arrives should be treated with caution. Future work could extend the estimator to instrumental variables and dynamic panels, apply it to firm- and region-level data on the intensity of digital use, and examine the moderating roles of institutions, competition and data governance, all of which are central to the digital economy.

\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}

Throughout, the year effect is a function of the time index $t$, which is an element of $F_{it}$, so $\lambda_t$ is absorbed by $\ell$ and $\E[\lambda_t\mid F_{it}]=\lambda_t$. The two-way operator $\M$ is the orthogonal projection onto the complement of the space spanned by country and year indicators; it is symmetric and idempotent, and $\M c=0$ for any vector of the form $c_{it}=c_i+c_t$. For country $i$, collect the $T_i$ observations into $Y_i$, $\Phi_i$ ($T_i\times K$) and $F_i$, and write $V_i=\Phi_i-m_0(F_i)$ and $e_i=Y_i-\ell_0(F_i)-V_i\beta_0$. Under Assumptions~\ref{as:exo}--\ref{as:cre} and $r_K=0$, $e_{it}=a_i+\varepsilon_{it}$ with $a_i=\alpha_i-h(\bar X_i,\bar D_i)$. In the asymptotic arguments below, the within transformation is treated as country demeaning $Q_i=I_{T_i}-T_i^{-1}\iota\iota'$; the additional year demeaning is asymptotically negligible because year means converge to their population counterparts at rate $G^{-1/2}$ and $\lambda_t$ is already absorbed by $\ell$.

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:orth}}
(i) For directions $\delta_\ell(F)$ and $\delta_m(F)$,
\[
\psi(\beta_0;\ell_0+r\delta_\ell,m_0+r\delta_m)=\E\big[(V_i-r\delta_{m,i})'Q_i\big(e_i-r\delta_{\ell,i}+r\delta_{m,i}\beta_0\big)\big].
\]
Differentiating at $r=0$ gives $-\E[\delta_{m,i}'Q_ie_i]+\E[V_i'Q_i(\delta_{m,i}\beta_0-\delta_{\ell,i})]$. In the first term, $Q_ie_i=Q_i\varepsilon_i$ because $a_i$ is constant within country, and $\E[\varepsilon_{it}\mid F_{i1},\dots,F_{iT_i}]=0$ by Assumption~\ref{as:exo}, since $F_{is}$ is a function of $(D_i,X_i)$; hence the term is zero. In the second term, $Q_iw_i$ is a function of $(F_{i1},\dots,F_{iT_i})$ for $w_i=\delta_{m,i}\beta_0-\delta_{\ell,i}$, and $\E[V_{it}\mid F_{i1},\dots,F_{iT_i}]=0$ by Assumption~\ref{as:suff}; hence it is also zero.

(ii) $\M(Y-\ell-c)=\M(Y-\ell)-\M c=\M(Y-\ell)$ and $\M(\Phi-m-d)=\M(\Phi-m)$ whenever $c$ and each column of $d$ are sums of country and year constants, because $\M$ annihilates the span of the country and year indicators. The sample and population moments are therefore unchanged for every $\beta$. \qed

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:struct}}
If $Z_{it}$ is a coordinate of $F_{it}$, then $b_k(Z_{it})$ is $F_{it}$-measurable and $\E[D_{it}b_k(Z_{it})\mid F_{it}]=b_k(Z_{it})\E[D_{it}\mid F_{it}]$. Consequently $\Phi_{it}-m(F_{it})=b(Z_{it})\{D_{it}-\mu(F_{it})\}$. In the cross-fitted version, replacing $\mu$ by $\hat\mu^{(-l)}$ gives the residuals in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dose}. \qed

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:an}}
Write $\tilde\Phi=\M(\Phi-\hat m)$, $\tilde Y=\M(Y-\hat\ell)$, and let $\tilde\Phi^0$, $\tilde Y^0$ be the same objects evaluated at the true nuisance functions. From~\eqref{eq:pls},
\[
\sqrt G(\hat\beta_\lambda-\beta_K)=\Big(\tfrac1G\tilde\Phi'\tilde\Phi+\lambda\tfrac nGP\Big)^{-1}\Big\{\tfrac1{\sqrt G}\tilde\Phi'(\tilde Y-\tilde\Phi\beta_K)-\sqrt G\lambda\tfrac nGP\beta_K\Big\}.
\]
The last term vanishes because $n/G\le\bar T$ and $\lambda_G\sqrt G\to0$. Let $\Delta_{m}=\hat m-m_0$ and $\Delta_\ell=\hat\ell-\ell_0$ on the evaluation fold. Then
\[
\tfrac1{\sqrt G}\tilde\Phi'(\tilde Y-\tilde\Phi\beta_K)=\tfrac1{\sqrt G}\sum_iV_i'Q_ie_i+R_1+R_2+R_3,
\]
where
\begin{align*}
R_1&=-G^{-1/2}\textstyle\sum_i\Delta_{m,i}'Q_ie_i, \qquad R_2=G^{-1/2}\textstyle\sum_iV_i'Q_i(\Delta_{m,i}\beta_K-\Delta_{\ell,i}),\\
R_3&=-G^{-1/2}\textstyle\sum_i\Delta_{m,i}'Q_i(\Delta_{m,i}\beta_K-\Delta_{\ell,i}).
\end{align*} Because the nuisance functions are estimated on countries outside the evaluation fold, conditionally on the training sample the summands of $R_1$ and $R_2$ are independent across countries with mean zero (by the arguments in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:orth}), and their conditional variances are bounded by a constant times $\|\Delta_m\|_2^2$ and $\|\Delta_m\|_2^2+\|\Delta_\ell\|_2^2$; hence $R_1,R_2=o_p(1)$ by Chebyshev's inequality under Assumption~\ref{as:reg}(b) and the consistency of both nuisance estimators. By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality and $\|Q_i\|\le1$, $\|R_3\|\le C\sqrt G\,\|\Delta_m\|_2(\|\Delta_m\|_2+\|\Delta_\ell\|_2)$ for a constant $C$ depending on $\bar T$, $\beta_K$ and the bounded basis, which is $o_p(1)$ under Assumption~\ref{as:reg}(b); by Proposition~\ref{prop:struct}, $\|\Delta_m\|_2$ is bounded by a constant times $\|\hat\mu-\mu\|_2$. By the same arguments, $G^{-1}\tilde\Phi'\tilde\Phi=G^{-1}\sum_iV_i'Q_iV_i+o_p(1)\xrightarrow{p}J$. The Lindeberg--Feller central limit theorem for independent clusters with bounded fourth moments gives $G^{-1/2}\sum_iV_i'Q_ie_i\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\Omega)$, since $V_i'Q_ie_i=V_i'Q_i\varepsilon_i=s_i$ and $\E[s_i]=0$. Slutsky's theorem yields the stated limit. Consistency of $G\hat V$ follows by standard arguments for cluster-robust variance estimators \citep{cameron2015cluster}, because $\hat u_{it}-u_{it}$ and $\tilde\Phi_{it}-\tilde\Phi^0_{it}$ vanish in mean square. Finally, for a fixed number $S$ of sample splits, each $\hat\beta_s$ satisfies the same linear representation with the same leading term, so the median $\hat\beta$ does too, and the variance adjustment is asymptotically negligible \citep{chernozhukov2018dml}. \qed

\paragraph{Implementation}
The nuisance learners are LightGBM gradient-boosted trees \citep{ke2017lightgbm} with 15 leaves, a minimum of 20 observations per leaf (10 in the simulations), row and column subsampling of 80\% and a learning rate of 0.03; the random-forest robustness check uses 400 trees \citep{breiman2001rf} with a minimum leaf size of 5 and half of the features considered at each split. The feature set $F_{it}$ contains the lagged controls, their country means, the country means of the treatment and the moderator, and the calendar year. The B-spline knots are placed at equally spaced quantiles of the moderator between its 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles, and values outside this range are assigned to the boundary. The GCV grid contains zero and 36 values of $\lambda$ equally spaced on the log scale between $10^{-4}$ and $10^{3}$. Simultaneous bands use 20,000 Gaussian draws. With two nuisance functions, five folds and five repetitions, a full Panel-DOSE fit on the empirical sample takes about 30 seconds on a single CPU core; by Proposition~\ref{prop:struct}, re-estimating the final stage with a different sieve takes a fraction of a second. The Python implementation, the data and the scripts that reproduce every table and figure are part of the replication package.
